🔬 Fundamental Principles

UV-Vis spectroscopy measures the absorption of ultraviolet and visible light by molecules. When light passes through a solution, molecules with appropriate energy gaps absorb photons at specific wavelengths, causing electronic transitions from ground state (S₀) to excited states (S₁, S₂, etc.). This absorption follows the Beer-Lambert Law, which states that absorbance is directly proportional to both the concentration and path length of the sample.

Key Concept: The wavelength of maximum absorption (λmax) is characteristic of a specific chromophore and depends on the energy difference between electronic states (ΔE = hc/λ).

🔧 Spectrophotometer Design & Components

Basic Spectrophotometer Configuration

A spectrophotometer consists of five main components working together to measure light absorption:

💡 Light Source
UV Region (200-400 nm): Deuterium (D₂) lamp produces continuous spectrum

Visible Region (400-750 nm): Tungsten-halogen lamp provides stable output

Alternative: Xenon flash lamps for high-intensity applications
🔍 Monochromator
Separates white light into discrete wavelengths using either:

Diffraction Grating: Most common, wide wavelength range

Prism: Higher resolution but narrower range

Controls wavelength selection via stepper motor
🧪 Sample Cuvette
UV Applications: Quartz (SiO₂) or fused silica (transparent to 190 nm)

Visible Applications: Plastic, glass, or disposable cuvettes

Standard Path Length: 1.0 cm (must be consistent)

Practical Tip: Always check optical quality before use
📡 Detector Systems
Photomultiplier Tube (PMT): High sensitivity, wide range, UV-Vis optimal

Photodiode Array: Simultaneous multi-wavelength detection

CCD Detector: Modern instruments, excellent linearity

Sensitivity: ppm to μM range possible
Important: Single-beam instruments require baseline correction before each measurement. Double-beam instruments automatically compensate by comparing sample and reference simultaneously, reducing errors from light source fluctuations.

📈 Protein Quantification by A₂₈₀

The absorption at 280 nm is primarily due to aromatic amino acids (Trp and Tyr). This method is fast and non-destructive:

Concentration (mg/mL) = (A₂₈₀) / (ε × b)

Where ε varies by protein (typically 0.5-2.0 for common proteins) Extinction coefficient calculated from amino acid composition

🧬 DNA/RNA Purity Assessment

Two key ratios determine nucleic acid purity and contamination:

A₂₆₀/A₂₈₀ Ratio
Pure DNA: ~1.8
Pure RNA: ~2.0

Lower ratio indicates: Protein contamination (absorbs at 280 nm)

Acceptable range: 1.7-1.9 for DNA, 1.9-2.1 for RNA
A₂₆₀/A₂₃₀ Ratio
Pure DNA: ~2.0-2.2
Pure RNA: ~2.0-2.2

Lower ratio indicates: Carbohydrate/phenol contamination

Typical range: 2.0-2.2 is optimal

⚙️ Experimental Best Practices

🔧 Baseline Correction & Calibration

1
Blank/Baseline Measurement: Measure only solvent (buffer/water) first. Set this as reference (100% transmittance)
2
Optical Density Check: Verify cuvette optical quality. Scratches or contamination cause high scatter
3
Temperature Equilibration: Allow sample 15 min to reach instrument temperature (usually 25°C)
4
Wavelength Calibration: Use standard (e.g., potassium dichromate) weekly to verify accuracy

📊 Absorbance Range for Accuracy

The Beer-Lambert Law is only linear within specific absorbance ranges due to instrumental limitations and scattering effects:

Critical Rule: Always dilute samples with high absorbance (>2.0) to bring them into the linear range. A₁ = 2.0 is the absolute maximum; beyond this, Beer's Law breaks down due to scattering and instrument limitations.

⚠️ Common Errors & Troubleshooting

High Absorbance Reading: Check if cuvette is scratched, contaminated, or contains precipitate. Clean with distilled water and ethanol.
Noisy Baseline: Allow instrument 20 min warm-up time. Check lamp stability and detector sensitivity settings.
Inconsistent Results: Verify temperature stability (±0.5°C variation causes errors). Ensure cuvette orientation is identical each measurement.
Wavelength Shift: Perform calibration with reference standard. Deuterium lamp may need replacement if older than 1000 hours.

🔬 Advanced Concepts

Electronic Transitions

UV-Vis spectroscopy involves π→π* and n→π* transitions. The energy required depends on the conjugation and orbital overlap:

ΔE = E(excited) - E(ground) = hc/λ = hν

Higher conjugation → Lower energy gap → Longer wavelength absorption Example: Ethene (λmax ~165 nm) vs. 1,3-Butadiene (λmax ~217 nm)

Solvent Effects (Bathochromism/Hypsochromism)

Solvent polarity significantly affects λmax due to stabilization of electronic states:

Red Shift (Bathochromism)
Polar solvents stabilize excited states more than ground states

Results in lower energy gap → longer λmax

Example: Moving from hexane to methanol increases λmax
Blue Shift (Hypsochromism)
Hydrogen bonding or specific solvation stabilizes ground state

Results in higher energy gap → shorter λmax

Example: Phenol shows pH-dependent shifts
Practical Impact: Always use the same solvent for comparison studies. Buffer composition, pH, and ionic strength can shift absorption peaks by 2-5 nm, leading to erroneous conclusions.

Deviations from Beer's Law

Several factors cause non-linearity:

Chemical Deviations
Dissociation: Weak acid/base showing absorbance changes with concentration

Aggregation: Dyes form dimers/polymers at high concentration

Complexation: Formation of new absorbing species
Instrumental Deviations
Stray Light: Non-monochromatic light causes curve flattening

Limited Bandwidth: Wide slits reduce resolution

Detector Non-linearity: PMT saturation at high intensity

✓
Biological Applications: Protein quantification at 280 nm, nucleic acid analysis at 260 nm, and real-time enzyme kinetics monitoring.
