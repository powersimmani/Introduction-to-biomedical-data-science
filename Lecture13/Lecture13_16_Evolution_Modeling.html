Sequence Evolution Models

Molecular sequence evolution follows probabilistic models that describe how DNA, RNA, and protein sequences change over time through substitutions, insertions, and deletions. Understanding these models is fundamental to reconstructing evolutionary history, estimating divergence times, and inferring selection pressures on biological sequences across different lineages.

Essential Principles of Substitution Models:

  • Time-reversibility: Probability of A→G equals G→A at equilibrium, simplifying likelihood calculations significantly
  • Markov property: Future states depend only on current state, independent of evolutionary history
  • Rate matrix Q: Describes instantaneous substitution rates; diagonal elements ensure rows sum to zero
  • Transition probability: Calculated via matrix exponential P(t) = e^(Qt), giving probability of change over time t
  • Among-site variation: Critical for realism; sites evolve at different rates due to functional constraints
  • Model selection: Use likelihood ratio tests or information criteria (AIC/BIC) to balance fit and complexity
Jukes-Cantor Distance:
d = -3/4 × ln(1 - 4p/3)
Kimura 2-Parameter Distance:
d = -1/2 × ln[(1-2P-Q)√(1-2Q)]
where P = transition proportion, Q = transversion proportion

Application Example: HIV Evolution

HIV-1 exhibits extremely rapid evolution with substitution rates around 10⁻³ per site per year, approximately a million times faster than nuclear DNA. Codon models reveal strong positive selection (ω > 1) in envelope genes allowing immune escape, while structural genes show purifying selection (ω < 1). The gamma distribution shape parameter α ≈ 0.5 indicates extreme rate heterogeneity, with many invariant sites and some hypervariable regions.

Phylogenetic Inference

Phylogenetic inference reconstructs evolutionary relationships among organisms or genes from molecular sequence data. Different computational approaches balance statistical rigor with computational efficiency, navigating the vast space of possible tree topologies through distance-based clustering, parsimony optimization, maximum likelihood estimation, or Bayesian posterior sampling.

Key Concepts in Phylogenetic Inference:

  • Tree space size: Number of possible trees grows super-exponentially: (2n-5)!! unrooted trees for n taxa
  • Heuristic search: Use tree rearrangements (NNI, SPR, TBR) to explore tree space without exhaustive enumeration
  • Bootstrap support: Resample alignment columns 100-1000 times to quantify confidence in each clade
  • Model selection: Choose substitution model using likelihood ratio tests (nested models) or AIC/BIC (non-nested)
  • Molecular clock: Test for rate constancy; if violated, use relaxed clock or local clock models
  • Bayesian advantage: Provides posterior probabilities and accounts for phylogenetic uncertainty naturally
Log-Likelihood of Tree T:
ln L(T) = Σ_i ln[Σ_x π_x ∏_edges P_xy(t)]
Bootstrap Proportion:
BP = (replicates supporting clade) / (total replicates)
BP ≥ 70% typically considered reliable support

Case Study: Bird Phylogeny

Whole-genome analyses of 48 bird species using maximum likelihood with partitioned models (separate parameters for different gene classes) resolved long-standing debates. Bootstrap support exceeded 95% for most nodes. Key findings included neoaves radiation occurring rapidly after the K-Pg extinction event 66 MYA, explaining why earlier studies with limited data failed to resolve relationships. This demonstrates how ML methods with appropriate models can extract phylogenetic signal even from rapid radiations.

Ancestral Sequence Reconstruction

Ancestral sequence reconstruction infers the most likely sequences at internal nodes of a phylogenetic tree, allowing us to resurrect ancient proteins, study evolutionary pathways, and understand how molecular functions evolved over deep time. These methods combine phylogenetic models with probabilistic inference to estimate extinct sequences with quantifiable uncertainty.

Ancestral Reconstruction Principles:

  • Joint vs. marginal: Joint reconstruction finds optimal states for all nodes simultaneously; marginal calculates probability for each node independently
  • Uncertainty quantification: Likelihood methods provide posterior probabilities; essential for assessing reconstruction confidence
  • Ambiguity handling: When multiple states have similar probabilities, synthesize and test alternative reconstructions
  • Gap treatment: Indels complicate reconstruction; typically handled by excluding gap-containing sites or using indel models
  • Model dependency: Reconstructions sensitive to substitution model choice; use model averaging or test multiple models
  • Experimental validation: Synthesizing ancestral proteins tests reconstruction accuracy and reveals ancient molecular properties
Marginal Posterior Probability for State x at Node n:
P(x_n | Data) = Σ_(-n) P(x_n, x_(-n), Tree | Data)
where x_(-n) represents all other ancestral states

Application: Steroid Receptor Evolution

Ancestral reconstruction of steroid receptors revealed how hormone specificity evolved. The ancestral corticoid receptor (reconstructed at ~450 MYA) responded to both estrogens and corticosteroids. Just two amino acid substitutions shifted specificity to estrogen-only binding in the estrogen receptor lineage, demonstrating how protein function can evolve through minimal sequence changes. This work combined computational reconstruction with experimental resurrection to map the evolutionary trajectory of hormone signaling.

Coevolution Analysis

Coevolution occurs when evolutionary changes in one molecular entity are correlated with changes in another, due to structural, functional, or fitness interactions. Detecting coevolutionary signals allows us to predict protein contacts, identify functionally important residues, and understand molecular interactions that drive coordinated evolution across genes, proteins, or even entire genomes.

Coevolution Detection Principles:

  • Mutual information: Measures reduction in uncertainty about one position given another; simple but includes indirect correlations
  • Direct coupling: DCA and similar methods separate direct from indirect evolutionary couplings using global statistical models
  • Phylogenetic correction: Account for shared ancestry to avoid spurious correlations from tree structure
  • Alignment depth: Require thousands of homologous sequences for reliable coevolution signals
  • Mirrortree analysis: Compares phylogenetic trees of different genes to detect functional interactions
  • Statistical significance: Use permutation tests or null models to assess significance of observed correlations
Mutual Information (MI) between positions i and j:
MI(i,j) = ΣΣ P(a_i, b_j) log[P(a_i, b_j) / (P(a_i)P(b_j))]
DCA Score (direct coupling):
DI_ij = √(Σ_ab e_ij(a,b)²)
where e_ij are direct coupling parameters from maximum entropy model

Success Story: AlphaFold and Coevolution

AlphaFold2's breakthrough in protein structure prediction heavily relies on coevolutionary information extracted from multiple sequence alignments. The model learns spatial constraints from coevolving residue pairs identified through attention mechanisms that capture both direct and higher-order correlations. This demonstrates how coevolution analysis, combined with deep learning, can solve one of biology's grand challenges—predicting 3D structure from sequence alone with near-experimental accuracy.

Fitness Landscapes

Fitness landscapes represent the relationship between genotypes or phenotypes and their reproductive success. These multidimensional spaces help us understand evolutionary trajectories, constraints on adaptation, and the accessibility of beneficial mutations through the complex topology of sequence space.

Key Concepts:

  • Fitness Peaks: Genotypes with maximum fitness in their local neighborhood; populations tend to evolve toward peaks
  • Fitness Valleys: Low-fitness regions between peaks that can trap populations and prevent access to higher peaks
  • Ruggedness: The complexity of the landscape; rugged landscapes have many local optima
  • Epistasis: Interactions between mutations where the effect of one depends on genetic background
  • Sign Epistasis: When a mutation is beneficial in one background but deleterious in another
  • Evolutionary Accessibility: Which genotypes can be reached through single mutational steps
  • Adaptive Walks: Trajectories through sequence space following fitness gradients
Wright-Fisher Model for Fixation Probability:
P(fixation) ≈ (1 - e^(-2Ns)) / (1 - e^(-4Ns))
where N = population size, s = selection coefficient

Example: Antibiotic Resistance Evolution

Studies of beta-lactamase evolution revealed a rugged fitness landscape where the path to high-level antibiotic resistance requires crossing fitness valleys. Some highly resistant variants can only be accessed through specific mutational paths involving 5+ mutations, explaining why certain resistance mechanisms emerge more frequently than others in clinical settings. Sign epistasis between mutations means that intermediate genotypes have lower fitness, creating evolutionary constraints.
